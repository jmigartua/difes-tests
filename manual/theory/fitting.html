Least-squares fitting

The objective, the optimiser, bounds and frozen parameters, the Jacobian, the uncertainties and what they mean, and the expert’s strategy.

The objective

Given spectra y_{t,i} measured at wavenumbers \omega_{t,i} with uncertainties \sigma_{t,i}, and a model f_t(\omega; \mathbf p) for each (the quantity of the Optics page for the sample’s geometry, computed from the dielectric function of the Models page), the fitter minimises the weighted sum of squares

\chi^2(\mathbf p) = \sum_t w_t \sum_i \left(\frac{y_{t,i} - f_t(\omega_{t,i};\,\mathbf p)}{\sigma_{t,i}}\right)^2

over the vector \mathbf p of free parameters: every parameter of every model reachable from the fit terms that is not frozen, each counted once even when shared (fit.fit). When the errors are independent and Gaussian, minimising \chi^2 is maximum likelihood; when \sigma is unknown and set to 1, it is ordinary least squares and the absolute scale of the uncertainties is recovered afterwards from the residuals.

The term weights w_t are the user’s; their use is to balance spectra of different kinds or point densities (a reflectivity with 4000 points and an emissivity with 1200 of the same sample), not to replace uncertainties.

The optimiser

difes uses the trust-region reflective algorithm (Branch, Coleman & Li 1999) as implemented in scipy.optimize.least_squares, a Levenberg–Marquardt-type method for problems with bounds. At each iteration it solves the linearised problem \min\|J\,\delta + \mathbf r\|^2 within a trust region and reflects steps that would cross a bound, so that every iterate stays inside the box [\mathbf l, \mathbf u]. Convergence is declared on the relative change of the cost (ftol), of the parameters (xtol) or of the gradient (gtol), all at 10^{-8} by default; the report prints the condition met. The pure Levenberg–Marquardt method (method="lm") is available for unbounded problems only and is switched to TRF automatically when any bound is finite.

Bounds. Every parameter carries [l, u]; the defaults keep frequencies, dampings and amplitudes non-negative and \varepsilon_\infty \ge 0 (passivity), and the user narrows them to keep a weak oscillator near its starting position. A parameter that ends a fit exactly on a bound is not determined by the data in that direction; its error is not meaningful.

Frozen parameters are removed from \mathbf p altogether, so the problem is smaller and the remaining Jacobian better conditioned. This is the mechanism of the guided strategy below.

Passivity of the four-parameter model is added as a residual \lambda\max(0, \sum_j(\gamma_{TO,j} - \gamma_{LO,j})) per model, \lambda = 10^3 by default: a soft constraint that vanishes when the sum is non-negative.

The Jacobian

J_{ki} = \partial r_k/\partial p_i, with r_k = \sqrt{w_t}(f - y)/\sigma the scaled residuals. Two sources:

  • analytic, when every term of every model has analytic \partial\chi/\partial p (Drude, Lorentz, Gaussian) and every fit term is a bulk R, E, \varepsilon or \chi spectrum: \partial R/\partial p = \mathrm{Re}[g\,\partial\varepsilon/\partial p] with g = 2\tilde r^*/(\tilde n(\tilde n + 1)^2) (derived on the Optics page), \partial E/\partial p = -\partial R/\partial p, \partial\varepsilon'/\partial p = \mathrm{Re}\,\partial\varepsilon/\partial p, and so on;
  • finite differences (two-point, scipy’s step selection) otherwise: slabs, films, four-parameter modes, expression terms.

The analytic Jacobian is exact and cheaper by a factor of the number of parameters per function evaluation; the two agree to the finite-difference accuracy, which is a test (--fd forces the comparison).

Uncertainties

At the optimum \mathbf p^* the residuals are linearised, \mathbf r \approx \mathbf r^* + J\,\delta\mathbf p, and the parameter covariance is

\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf p) = \left(J^\mathsf T J\right)^{-1}\;\chi^2_{red}, \qquad \chi^2_{red} = \frac{\chi^2(\mathbf p^*)}{N - m},

with N the total number of points and m the number of free parameters (FitResult.covariance, via the pseudo-inverse). The reported errors are the square roots of the diagonal, one standard deviation. Two remarks on the scaling by \chi^2_{red}:

  • when the \sigma_{t,i} are realistic, \chi^2_{red} \approx 1 and the scaling changes nothing; the errors are those of the data propagated through the fit;
  • when \sigma was set to 1 (no uncertainty column) or is badly estimated, the scaling replaces the assumed noise by the observed residual, so the errors remain meaningful as the spread that would arise if the fit were repeated with the same residual level. This is the classical prescription (Press et al.) and what most fitting programs, FOCUS included, report.

What the errors do not say: whether the model is right (a smooth systematic residual gives small errors and a wrong model), and whether parameters are degenerate beyond what the diagonal shows. The full covariance is available for the second question; a relative error of hundreds of percent, or a parameter on a bound, is the usual symptom (the two Drude terms of pure chromium, whose plasma frequencies and dampings compensate over the measured range).

The report’s rms residual per term, in the units of the spectrum, is the honest measure of the fit’s quality; reduced \chi^2 near 1 says the uncertainties are consistent with it.

Several spectra, shared parameters

A fit term binds one spectrum to one model and one geometry (thickness, coherence, substrate). Several terms may point to the same model object (a reflectivity and an emissivity of the same sample), or to different models that share some parameter objects through link_parameters (a temperature series with a common \varepsilon_\infty, or a film whose substrate model is fitted at the same time). The fitter collects the unique free parameter objects wherever they occur, so the sharing is respected without any extra syntax; fit() works on deep copies that preserve the sharing unless inplace=True.

The expert’s strategy

Least squares converges to the nearest local minimum, and a dielectric-function model with a dozen oscillators has many. The strategy that FOCUS’s users follow, and the session graph is designed to record, is a guided fit:

  1. Start from a model whose \varepsilon_\infty and oscillator positions are read off the spectrum (positions at the minima of E or maxima of R; \varepsilon_\infty from the reflectivity above the phonons, R_\infty = ((\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty} - 1)/(\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty} + 1))^2).
  2. Freeze everything but \varepsilon_\infty and the strengths; fit.
  3. Free the positions; fit. Free the dampings; fit.
  4. Free everything; fit; look at the residual. A structured residual is a missing term (add one where the residual has a shape) or a wrong geometry (a film where a bulk was assumed), not a reason for more iterations.
  5. Check passivity (\varepsilon'' \ge 0), the static constant against any known value, and, when a Kramers–Kronig phase is available, the model’s phase against it.

The uncertainties of the last step are the ones to quote.

    What this is

    The test ledger of difes, a program for dielectric-function models, reflectivity, emissivity, Kramers–Kronig and fitting of infrared spectra, built as a personal project to give the group an open and documented counterpart of FOCUS. Every check the program has passed through is listed here with its grade, its data and its result, next to the validation pages the checks come from and the complete manual: how the app looks and works, and the physics it implements, equation by equation.

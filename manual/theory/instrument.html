The measurement

What the Fourier-transform spectrometer does before a spectrum exists, and what a measured emissivity is. Not yet modelled by difes; stated here because a fit is only as good as the spectrum’s provenance.

From interferogram to spectrum

A Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer records, with a Michelson interferometer whose moving mirror scans an optical path difference x, the interferogram

I(x) = \int_0^\infty B(\omega)\left[1 + \cos(2\pi\omega x)\right]d\omega ,

and the spectrum B(\omega) is the cosine transform of I(x) - \bar I. Four operations separate the recorded interferogram from the spectrum that the program reads (Griffiths & de Haseth 2007):

  • Truncation and apodisation. The scan has a maximum path difference L, so the transform is convolved with the instrument line shape 2L\,\mathrm{sinc}(2\pi\omega L) of width \approx 1/(2L): the spectral resolution. Multiplying the interferogram by a window (triangular, Happ–Genzel, Blackman–Harris, Norton–Beer) trades resolution for the suppression of the sinc’s side lobes; the resolution of a spectrum in \mathrm{cm}^{-1} is the number to know when a fringe pattern or a sharp phonon is analysed.
  • Zero filling. Extending the interferogram with zeros before the transform interpolates the spectrum on a finer grid; it adds points, not information. A spectrum with a point spacing much finer than its resolution has correlated points, and a reduced \chi^2 computed with them is not a count of independent degrees of freedom.
  • Phase correction (Mertz). Optical and electronic asymmetries make the interferogram asymmetric, so its transform is complex; the Mertz method estimates the slowly varying phase from a short double-sided portion around the centre burst, and rotates the full transform by it. An uncorrected or badly corrected phase appears as a baseline distortion and as negative intensities.
  • Ratioing. A single-beam spectrum is the product of the source, the beam splitter, the detector and the atmosphere with the sample’s response; the quantity of physics is the ratio of two single beams, sample over reference, which cancels the instrument function only in so far as it did not drift between the two.

The 2023 notebook in the diagnosis implements these steps for teaching; difes reads only the final spectrum (kind="raw" for single beams and interferograms, so that they can be stored and plotted, not fitted).

Reflectivity

A reflectivity is the ratio of the single beam reflected by the sample to that reflected by a reference of known reflectivity (gold or aluminium mirror in the infrared), times the reference’s reflectivity:

R_{sample}(\omega) = \frac{S_{sample}(\omega)}{S_{ref}(\omega)}\,R_{ref}(\omega) .

Near-normal incidence (10° to 12°) is the usual geometry; at that angle the s and p reflectivities differ from the normal one by less than 10^{-3} for the materials of interest, which is why difes’s optics are normal-incidence.

Emissivity

The group’s measurements are of spectral emissivity at temperature: the sample is heated (by a \mathrm{CO_{2}} laser in CEMHTI’s setup, by a furnace elsewhere) and its thermal radiance is compared with that of a blackbody at the same temperature. With S the measured single beams and S_{bg} the surroundings,

E(\omega, T) = \frac{S_{sample}(\omega, T) - S_{bg}(\omega)}{S_{bb}(\omega, T) - S_{bg}(\omega)} ,

a ratio in which the instrument function cancels, up to the assumptions that the sample and the blackbody are at the same temperature, that the surroundings’ contribution is the same in both, and that the blackbody’s emissivity is one. The Planck function that gives the blackbody radiance in wavenumber units is in the built-in library as Planck_WN(x, T) (FOCUS’s form, 1.191\cdot10^{-16}\,\omega^3/(e^{1.4388\,\omega/T} - 1) in W m^{-2} sr^{-1} per \mathrm{cm}^{-1} with \omega in \mathrm{cm}^{-1} and T in K).

By Kirchhoff’s law the normal spectral emissivity equals the normal absorptivity, 1 - R for an opaque sample and 1 - R - T for a semitransparent one, at the temperature of the measurement; so an emissivity is fitted with the same dielectric-function models as a reflectivity, with the parameters those of the hot sample. The group’s files carry an uncertainty column, the standard deviation of repeated scans; that is the \sigma of the fitting page, and its realism is what makes reduced \chi^2 near 1 meaningful.

Two consequences for the analysis:

  • The temperature is a property of the spectrum (difes records it) and, across a series, of the parameters: phonon positions soften and dampings grow with T, which is the physics the semi-quantum models were built to describe. A fit of a series with linked parameters is the tool for it.
  • The sample’s temperature is known to a few kelvin at best; an error \delta T shifts the whole emissivity by roughly \delta T/T times the logarithmic derivative of Planck’s function, a smooth multiplicative distortion that a fit will absorb into \varepsilon_\infty or a broad term. A structured residual is not the only symptom of a wrong model; a slightly wrong scale is one too.

What difes does not do yet

The instrument module of the roadmap (apodisation, zero filling, phase correction, resolution convolution of a model spectrum before comparison with data, the angle of incidence) is future work; the design page lists it. Until then, spectra enter difes as processed by the spectrometer’s software, and the manual’s advice is: know the resolution, the apodisation and the angle of every file, and keep them in the spectrum’s metadata.

On this page

    What this is

    The test ledger of difes, a program for dielectric-function models, reflectivity, emissivity, Kramers–Kronig and fitting of infrared spectra, built as a personal project to give the group an open and documented counterpart of FOCUS. Every check the program has passed through is listed here with its grade, its data and its result, next to the validation pages the checks come from and the complete manual: how the app looks and works, and the physics it implements, equation by equation.
